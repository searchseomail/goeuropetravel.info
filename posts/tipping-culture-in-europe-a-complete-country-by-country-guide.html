Tipping Culture in Europe: A Complete Country-by-Country Guide

terra_nova_lite
15.07.2026 • Read time: 5 min

Navigating the nuances of tipping in Europe can be a source of anxiety for many travelers. Unlike the United States, where gratuity is often mandatory and calculated as a fixed percentage, European tipping culture is far more nuanced, varying significantly from one border to the next. Understanding these local customs is essential for a stress-free trip, ensuring you reward good service without overpaying or offending your hosts.

Before you embark on your journey, it is wise to consult the best travel apps to download for your Europe trip, as many of these tools now include currency converters and local etiquette guides that can help you calculate appropriate tips on the fly.

Understanding the European Approach to Gratuity

In most European countries, service staff are paid a living wage, and gratuity is viewed as an extra token of appreciation rather than a primary source of income. Service charges are frequently included in the bill, particularly in tourist-heavy areas. However, this does not mean tipping is unheard of. In many regions, rounding up the bill or leaving a small cash tip is considered polite for excellent service.

Tipping Etiquette by Region

To help you prepare, we have categorized the general expectations across the continent. Keep in mind that when planning your itinerary—whether you are exploring the classic route through Prague, Vienna, and Budapest, or venturing further afield—local customs should always be respected.

Western and Central Europe

  • France: A "service compris" (service included) is standard. While not required, leaving an extra €1–€2 or rounding up the bill at a café is common.
  • Germany: Tipping is expected but modest. Usually, you tell the waiter the total you wish to pay (including the tip) as you hand over the cash or card. If you are visiting 10 fairytale castles in Germany you must visit, expect to tip around 5-10% at sit-down restaurants.
  • Switzerland: Service is almost always included in the bill. While rounding up is appreciated, it is never mandatory.

Southern Europe and the Mediterranean

  • Italy: Look for "coperto" (cover charge) on your receipt. If this is present, no further tip is expected. For exceptional service, a few extra euros are appreciated.
  • Spain: Tipping is not deeply ingrained. Leaving small change after a meal is acceptable, but not leaving a tip is perfectly normal.
  • Croatia: If you are planning a trip to the coast, you might find this guide to the islands of Croatia helpful; in restaurants here, a 5-10% tip is standard for good service.

Northern Europe and the British Isles

  • United Kingdom: In restaurants, a 10-12.5% service charge is often added to the bill. If it is included, you do not need to tip further. If not, 10% is the standard.
  • Scandinavia: Tipping is rare. Service is included in the price, and staff are paid well. You might round up the bill in a taxi, but it is entirely optional.

Final Tips for the Savvy Traveler

Whether you are navigating the best routes for a road trip through the Scottish Highlands or enjoying a night out at the best bars in European capitals, always carry a small amount of local currency. While many places now accept card payments, adding a tip to a credit card terminal is not always possible in smaller establishments. Cash remains king when it comes to showing your appreciation for great service.

Ultimately, tipping is a personal choice. If the service was poor, do not feel obligated to tip. If the service was exceptional, a small gesture goes a long way in making the staff feel valued. Always observe what the locals are doing—if you see people leaving small change, follow their lead.
